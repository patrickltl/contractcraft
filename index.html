Guide

The freelance contract guide: what a professional agreement must include

Reading time ~5 minutes. Covers: freelance contract templates, independent contractor agreements, and payment terms that survive contact with real clients.

Why freelancers get paid late — or never

Non-payment is rarely a mystery. In most disputes between a freelancer and a client, both sides sincerely believe different things: the freelancer believes the third revision round was included in the fee, the client believes it wasn’t; the freelancer believes the handoff date was the 15th, the client remembers the 30th. A freelance contract exists to make those beliefs irrelevant. When the scope, the deliverables, the deadlines, and the payment terms are in a signed document, the conversation shifts from “he said, she said” to “section 3 says.” That shift alone resolves most disagreements, because clients who might quietly deprioritize an unpaid invoice behave very differently when the terms are written, specific, and acknowledged by signature.

What a freelance contract template should actually contain

Search for a “freelance contract template” and you’ll find everything from two-paragraph emails to 20-page law-firm documents. A useful freelance contract lives in between. It needs, at minimum: a clear description of the services; an itemized list of deliverables with dates; the fee structure (hourly or fixed) and currency; explicit payment terms; a stated number of included revision rounds with out-of-scope work handled by written change orders; a kill fee for mid-project cancellation; confidentiality protection; an intellectual property clause; independent contractor status; a termination mechanism with notice; and governing law. Everything else is refinement. The template ContractCraft generates contains all of these as numbered articles, with conditional sections included or removed based on your answers — turn confidentiality off, for example, and the numbering renumbers automatically with cross-references intact.

Freelance contract vs. independent contractor agreement

People use the terms interchangeably, and there is real overlap, but the emphasis differs. An independent contractor agreement is about the relationship: it establishes that you are not an employee, that you control how the work is performed, that you carry your own taxes and insurance, and that neither party can bind the other. A freelance services agreement is about the work: services, deliverables, revisions, and payment. If a client ever questions your status — or a tax agency does — the contractor-status clause is what matters. If a client ever questions the invoice, the services-and-payment articles are what matter. You need both halves, which is why the document ContractCraft generates opens as a freelance services agreement and contains a full independent contractor status article inside it.

Payment terms that actually get you paid

Payment terms are where contracts earn their keep. Three rules of thumb. First, shorter beats longer: Net 14 is a sensible default for freelance work, Net 7 for short engagements, and Net 30 is a concession you grant, not a starting point. Second, upfront money changes behavior: for fixed-fee projects, 50% before work begins is the single most effective anti-non-payment device available to a freelancer, because a client with a deposit at stake has skin in the game. Third, a late fee is a lever, not (usually) a revenue source: a simple interest rate per month on overdue amounts — 1.5% is common — both deters delay and gives you a principled, pre-agreed basis for the awkward conversation. ContractCraft bakes all three into its fees-and-payment article, including the right to pause work after ten days’ notice on an unpaid invoice.

Five mistakes that weaken an otherwise good contract

  • Signing before reading. A template you didn’t read protects you exactly as much as no contract when a clause contradicts your intention.
  • Vague deliverables. “Design work for the website” invites scope creep. “Final logo files (SVG/PNG/PDF), week 3” ends arguments.
  • Transferring IP too early. Never assign ownership before payment clears. IP-on-full-payment is the freelancer’s strongest clause.
  • No late-fee provision. After the fact you have no agreed basis for interest; before signing, you do.
  • Letting the client’s paper win by default. If a client sends their own agreement, compare it clause-by-clause against yours — especially liability caps, IP timing, and termination.

Is a generated contract enforceable?

A contract’s force comes from the agreement between the parties — offer, acceptance, consideration, and signatures — not from who typed it. Tools like this one exist in a well-established category: the same structure underlies the templates sold by legal-forms sites, contract platforms, and the thousand-copy marketplace listings freelancers buy every day. What a generator adds is consistency (numbered articles, defined terms, cross-references that renumber themselves) and speed. What it cannot do is know your jurisdiction’s specific quirks or your deal’s nuances. That is why the disclaimer stays attached to everything ContractCraft produces: this is a template tool, not legal advice. For high-value or unusual engagements, spend an hour with an attorney reviewing the generated draft — it makes that hour dramatically cheaper than drafting from scratch.

Honest answers

Is a freelance contract legally binding?

A contract becomes binding when both parties agree to its terms and sign it, whether on paper or electronically. ContractCraft produces the document; what makes it binding is the offer, acceptance, and consideration between you and your client. Clear terms and signatures are exactly why a written agreement beats a handshake.

What is the difference between a freelance contract and an independent contractor agreement?

They overlap heavily. An independent contractor agreement focuses on the working relationship (contractor status, tax responsibility, no employment benefits). A freelance services agreement focuses on the work itself (services, deliverables, revisions, payment). ContractCraft’s document does both: it opens as a freelance services agreement and contains a full independent contractor status clause.

Do I need a contract for small projects?

Small projects go wrong too, and a short signed agreement costs you minutes to create. Most freelancers who get stiffed were not doing big deals — they just had nothing in writing. Use your judgment for trivial favors, but any engagement with real money and real deadlines deserves a contract.

What payment terms should I use as a freelancer?

Net 14 is a reasonable default; Net 7 for short projects. For larger fixed-fee work, 50% upfront is the single most effective protection against non-payment — clients who pay a deposit rarely disappear. ContractCraft also lets you add a monthly late fee, which both deters late payment and gives you a clean basis for charging it.

What is a kill fee and why should my contract have one?

A kill fee compensates you when a client cancels mid-project after you reserved time for them. ContractCraft’s clause makes the client pay for all work performed plus a percentage (you choose; 25% is common) of the remaining fee. It filters out casual cancellations and keeps your calendar respected.

Who owns the work if the client hasn’t paid yet?

Under ContractCraft’s intellectual property clause, ownership of the final deliverables transfers to the client only upon full payment. Until then, you retain all rights and the client has no license to use the work. This single protection — IP assignment on payment — is the strongest anti-non-payment lever a freelance contract gives you.
